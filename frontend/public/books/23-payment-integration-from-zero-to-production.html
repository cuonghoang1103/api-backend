About this book

Taking money is the one feature where a bug has a unit of measurement. Everywhere else a mistake produces a wrong page or a slow response; here it produces a customer charged twice, an order shipped for free, or a shop that quietly stops recording sales. This book is about the handful of decisions that separate those outcomes, and it demonstrates each one against a working checkout.

The lab is a gateway and a merchant, both on your own machine, both speaking the real protocols. Chapter 2 signs a request and the gateway accepts it; change one space from + to %20 and it does not, which is the single most common reason a first integration fails. Chapter 3 delivers the same notification four times — once normally, once again, once with the amount edited, once pointing at another order — and only the first is allowed to change anything. Chapter 8 posts a settlement to a double-entry ledger and then posts one that does not balance, and watches it be rejected.

The idea running underneath all of it is in Chapter 0. A payment produces two messages that say the same thing: the customer’s browser comes back to your site, and the gateway calls your server directly. They look interchangeable and they are not. The first can be edited, and it may never arrive at all — the customer closes the tab, and as far as your shop is concerned they never paid. Only the second one may change a status. A shop that gets this backwards loses money in both directions at once.

Who should read this

Anyone about to accept money for the first time, and anyone maintaining a checkout they inherited. You need a terminal, some JavaScript and a sandbox account with either gateway; nothing here requires a real merchant agreement, and nothing here charges a real card.

How this book is organised

Chapters map one-to-one onto the ten modules of the Payment Integration track in Code Lab, each ending with that module’s graded exercises — 100 in total, all linked. Chapters 4 and 5 cover VNPay and PayOS specifically; the other eight apply to any gateway you will ever integrate.

The rule that prevents most payment bugs

Never trust a number that arrived from outside your system. Not the price in a form, not the total in a callback, not the amount in a return URL. Compute the amount yourself, store it, and compare everything that arrives against that record — the callback’s amount, its order reference, and whether you have already applied it. A signature proves a message was not altered in transit. It does not prove the message is about the order you think it is, for the amount you charged. That comparison is three lines of code and it closes most of the ways a checkout is actually attacked.

And the one that prevents the rest

Make the operation idempotent at the database, not in application logic. A unique constraint on the gateway’s reference turns “did we already process this?” from a question about timing — one your code will get wrong under concurrency — into a guarantee the database enforces. Gateways retry deliberately, so a duplicate notification is not an edge case: it is normal operation, and Chapter 6 shows the same one arriving four times.

Chapter Zero · Part I

Payment Fundamentals and Flows

Taking money is not a request that returns a result. It is a conversation between four parties over an unreliable network, in which the browser is the least trustworthy participant and the only one your code usually listens to. This chapter establishes who is who, and demonstrates the mistake that follows from getting that wrong.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Name the parties and what each one guarantees
  • Follow a redirect payment from order to settlement
  • Explain why the browser return decides nothing
  • Choose between hosted and embedded checkout
  • Run the lab used for the rest of this book

0.1 Who is involved

Table 0.1 The only party you talk to is the gateway, and everything below it is opaque — which matters because a failure at any level below arrives at you as one of a handful of codes. “Declined” may mean insufficient funds, a fraud rule, a bank outage, or a card that expired yesterday, and the gateway frequently cannot tell you which. Designing on the assumption that you will get a precise reason is designing for a system that does not exist.

Meanwhile a customer who pays and immediately closes the tab generates no return at all, and their perfectly good payment must still be recorded.

The bug this produces, in the order it happens

A shop marks orders paid in the return handler. It works in testing, because the tester always follows the redirect. In production three things happen. Customers who close the tab pay and never receive their goods — the money is in the account and the order sits pending forever. Somebody notices the URL contains vnp_ResponseCode=00 and tries editing it, and if the signature is not verified they get free goods. And because the return often arrives before the IPN, the two handlers race, which is why §0.2 shows the shop saying “thank you” while its own database still says pending.

Card data never entering your systems is what keeps you in the smallest PCI scope, and every step away from redirect multiplies the compliance work by more than it improves the experience. For the Vietnamese gateways this book targets the question barely arises — they are redirect-based by design, and the flow is bank transfer or QR rather than card entry at all.

And the flow is the same shape regardless of provider

Create an order, sign a request, redirect, verify a server-to-server callback, reconcile. VNPay signs with HMAC-SHA512 over sorted query parameters; PayOS signs with HMAC-SHA256 over sorted key=value pairs; Stripe signs a webhook body with a timestamped HMAC. The algorithms differ and the shape does not, which is why Chapters 3 to 5 can treat them as variations rather than as three separate integrations.

Those are the events worth watching, and they are invisible in an order table.

Checkpoint

Why must the browser return never change an order’s status?

Because it is delivered through the customer’s browser: they can edit the URL, and they can simply close the tab so it never arrives at all. A customer who pays and closes the tab must still get their goods. The return is for showing a message; the server-to-server IPN, verified and checked against your own record, is what changes state.

Why create the order before redirecting rather than after payment succeeds?

Because the order is what you verify the callback against — the amount, the currency, the fact that it exists at all. Creating it afterwards leaves nothing to compare to, nothing to reconcile, and no record of customers who started paying and did not finish.

What is the difference between authorisation and settlement?

Authorisation reserves the money and moves nothing; settlement actually transfers it, days later, in a batch. An authorisation expires, so an order authorised and never captured silently disappears. Vietnamese bank-transfer flows largely collapse the two, which removes that failure and means there is no void — only a refund.

Why does redirect checkout dominate for these gateways?

Because card data never touches your servers, which keeps you in the smallest PCI scope and removes most of the compliance work. VNPay and PayOS are redirect-based by design and use bank transfer or QR rather than card entry, so the trade-off that matters elsewhere barely arises.

Chapter One · Part I

Orders, Amounts and Currency

Money is not a number. It is an integer count of a unit, in a named currency, and the moment it becomes a floating-point value something is already wrong — it is only a question of how many transactions it takes to become visible. This chapter is about representing amounts correctly, and about the multiplication VNPay requires that looks like a bug and is not.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Represent money without losing any
  • Handle a currency with no minor unit
  • Send the amount the gateway expects
  • Design an order that can be reconciled
  • Round in the one place rounding belongs

1.1 Why floating point loses money

Represent money asVerdict
float / doubleNever — 0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3
integer minor unitsYes — cents, and for VND, dong
BigIntYes, once totals exceed 2^53
A decimal libraryYes, if the language lacks integers of the right size
NUMERIC(12,2) in the databaseYes — exact, and the DB enforces it
A stringFor transport only, never for arithmetic

Table 1.1 The second row is the standard answer and it needs one qualification for this book’s currency: the “minor unit” of VND is the dong. There are no hào in circulation, so 150,000 VND is stored as the integer 150000 — not as 15,000,000. That distinction becomes important in §1.2 for a reason that catches every VNPay integration exactly once.

1.2 The multiplication that looks like a bug

CurrencyMinor units1,000 units is stored as
VND01000
USD2100000 (cents)
EUR2100000
JPY01000
KWD31000000

Table 1.2 ISO 4217 assigns each currency an exponent, and VND’s is zero. Any code that assumes two decimal places — a helper that divides by 100 to display, a schema with NUMERIC(12,2), a library that formats cents — is wrong for VND by a factor of a hundred in whichever direction it guessed.

And PayOS does not do this

PayOS takes the amount in plain dong: an order of 150,000 VND is sent as amount: 150000. So a codebase integrating both gateways has two different conventions for the same field name, which is exactly the situation where a shared “amount” helper causes a hundred-fold error. Keep the conversion inside each gateway’s own adapter, where the convention is visible next to the code that depends on it.

1.3 An order that can be reconciled

Table 1.3 The last row is the cheapest protection in this book: a database-level unique constraint means a duplicate callback cannot be applied twice even if every layer of application logic above it is wrong. Chapter 6 builds on it; note here that it is a schema decision, made once, rather than a piece of code that must be correct everywhere.

And expires_at is not optional

Orders that are never resolved accumulate: the customer opened the payment page and closed it, the gateway session timed out, the callback never arrived. Without an expiry, “pending” becomes a bucket of thousands of rows nobody can distinguish between “abandoned” and “paid but we missed the callback” — and the second kind is a customer who paid and got nothing. Expire them on a schedule, and query the gateway before marking anything expired, which is Chapter 8.

1.4 Rounding, in one place

Table 1.4 The last row is a real bug and not an obvious one: formatting an amount for display, then parsing that string back to compute something, silently applies the display rounding to your arithmetic. The formatted value is an output. It never returns.

Checkpoint

Why must an amount never be a floating-point number?

Because binary floating point cannot represent most decimal fractions exactly — 0.1 + 0.2 !== 0.3 — so totals drift, comparisons fail, and the value sent to a gateway may not be the value stored. Use integer minor units. For VND the minor unit is the dong itself, so 150,000 VND is the integer 150000.

VND has no minor unit, so why does VNPay want the amount multiplied by 100?

Because it is a property of that field in that API, not a currency conversion. VNPay defines vnp_Amount as amount × 100 regardless of currency. Do the multiplication in the VNPay adapter only, and divide before comparing on the way back — PayOS takes plain dong, so a shared “amount” helper across both gateways is how a hundred-fold error happens.

Why separate orders from payment attempts?

Because one order can have several attempts — a failure, a retry, an expired session — and collapsing them destroys the history reconciliation depends on. It also gives you somewhere to record the gateway’s own reference and the raw callback, which is the only authoritative record of what you were told.

Round per line item or on the total?

On the total, once, and be consistent. Rounding each line and summing gives a different answer from rounding the sum, and the two disagree by a few units on some orders — which is precisely the discrepancy an accountant finds and nobody can explain. Formatted values are output only; never parse a displayed amount back into arithmetic.

Chapter Two · Part II

Redirect Checkout

A hosted checkout is a signed URL, a redirect, and two callbacks. Almost all of the work is in the signature — building it from exactly the right bytes, in exactly the right order, with exactly the right encoding — because a signature that is one character off produces the same rejection as an attack.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Build a payment request that the gateway accepts
  • Sign parameters exactly as the specification requires
  • Handle the encoding difference that breaks integrations
  • Verify a response before believing any of it
  • Handle the customer who never comes back

2.1 What gets signed, and in what order

2.2 The encoding detail that breaks integrations

How to debug an “invalid signature” that you cannot see

Log the exact string you hashed, not the parameters you built. Every difference lives in that string: an unencoded space, a trailing empty parameter, a key sorted with locale rules instead of byte order, a value that was a number where the gateway expects a string, or the hash field left in. Print signData beside the specification’s own worked example and the difference is visible in seconds; comparing objects instead can take an afternoon.

Table 2.1 The second row is the one that hides longest, because it only affects orders whose description contains a space, an accent, or an ampersand — which in Vietnamese order descriptions is most of them, and in the ASCII test data developers usually write, none of them.

Checkpoint

An integration returns “invalid signature” and every parameter looks right. What do you log?

The exact string you hashed, not the object you built. The difference is always in that string — an unencoded space, an empty parameter that should have been dropped, a locale-aware sort, or the hash field left in. Compare it against the specification’s worked example.

Why is a valid signature not enough to mark an order paid?

Because it only proves the gateway sent the message. It says nothing about whether the order is yours, whether the amount matches what you charged, or whether you already processed it. Check signature, then existence, then amount against your own record, then currency, then whether it is still pending.

Why compare signatures with a timing-safe function?

Because === returns as soon as bytes differ, so the time taken reveals how many leading characters were correct — which is enough to reconstruct a signature byte by byte. Compare lengths first, since the timing-safe comparison throws on mismatched lengths.

The gateway told the browser the payment succeeded but your IPN has not arrived. What should the page say?

That the payment is being confirmed, and refresh. Claiming “paid” asserts something you have not verified; claiming “failed” alarms a customer whose money has left their account. The race is normal — the return frequently arrives first.

Chapter Three · Part II

Webhooks and IPN Verification

The webhook is the only message in a payment flow that you can trust, and it arrives over a channel where anyone can send you anything. Everything in this chapter follows from that: verify before you read, compare against your own record, and assume it will arrive more than once.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Verify a webhook before acting on any of its contents
  • Reject the four ways a callback can be wrong
  • Respond in the way the gateway expects
  • Survive duplicates and retries
  • Receive webhooks reliably in production

3.1 Four deliveries, four outcomes

The callbackHandler mustResponse
Has a bad signatureReject and logInvalid signature
Names an order you do not haveRejectOrder not found
States an amount that differsRejectInvalid amount
Repeats one already appliedIgnore, successfullyAlready confirmed
Is valid and newApply, onceConfirm success
Cannot be processed right nowFail loudlySo it is retried

Table 3.1 Rows four and six look similar and are opposite. A duplicate must be acknowledged successfully, or the gateway will keep retrying something you have already done. A genuine failure — your database is down — must not be acknowledged, because the retry is the only thing that will save that payment. Answering “OK” to an event you failed to process is how money goes missing quietly.

3.2 Verify first, read second

And reject before you parse, not after

The body is attacker-controlled until the signature has been checked. Parsing it first means running a parser, and often a schema validator and an ORM query, on data from anyone who can reach your endpoint. Verifying first reduces that surface to one HMAC computation, and it is also the only order that makes the log line useful — “rejected a forged webhook from this address” rather than “something failed while processing an event”.

3.3 Answering the way the gateway expects

GatewayExpectsRetries when
VNPayJSON { RspCode, Message }RspCode is not 00
PayOSHTTP 200Non-2xx, or a timeout
StripeHTTP 2xxNon-2xx, or a timeout
MostA fast responseYou took too long

Table 3.2 VNPay is the odd one: it inspects a code in the body rather than the HTTP status, so returning 200 OK with RspCode: "97" means “I rejected this” and returning a 500 with no body means nothing useful at all. Read the specification for the exact codes — 00 confirm, 01 order not found, 02 already confirmed, 04 invalid amount, 97 invalid signature — and return them deliberately.

Acknowledge quickly, work afterwards

Gateways time out in seconds and treat a slow response as a failure, so a handler that sends an email, generates an invoice and calls a fulfilment API before responding will be retried while it is still working — and will then do all of it twice. Verify, record the event, acknowledge, and queue the rest. The handler’s only job is to durably record that the event happened; everything downstream is a separate, retryable job.

3.4 Receiving webhooks in production

Table 3.3 Row two surprises people and is correct: a webhook endpoint cannot require a session or a bearer token, because the caller is a machine that has neither. The HMAC signature is the authentication, which is exactly why every check in §3.2 matters — that one comparison is the whole of your access control on that route.

The endpoint that is unreachable during a deploy

Gateways retry, but not forever and not always in a way you can see. If your webhook endpoint returns 502 for the ninety seconds of a rolling deploy, some events land in the retry queue and some exhaust their attempts — and you find out weeks later during reconciliation. Two defences: return 200 as early as possible so the work happening afterwards cannot fail the acknowledgement, and reconcile against the gateway on a schedule so a missed webhook is detected rather than assumed absent. That is Chapter 8, and it is the reason it exists.

Checkpoint

A webhook arrives with a valid signature but names an order you do not have. What do you do?

Do not apply it, and acknowledge it successfully anyway — it may belong to another environment sharing the endpoint, and failing it invites permanent retries for something you will never accept. Log it. The four checks in order are signature, order exists, amount matches your record, and whether you have already handled it.

Signature verification works with curl and fails in production. Why?

A JSON body parser is re-serialising the body before you verify, and the re-serialised bytes are not the bytes that were signed — key order, whitespace and number formatting can all differ. Capture the raw body and verify on it, before any parsing middleware.

Why must a duplicate be acknowledged successfully but a database outage must not?

Because the acknowledgement controls retries. A duplicate has already been applied, so retrying achieves nothing and the gateway should stop. A failure has not been applied, and the retry is the only thing that will save that payment — acknowledging it discards the event silently.

Why does a webhook route have no authentication?

Because the caller is a machine with no session or token. The HMAC signature is the authentication, which is why it must be verified on raw bytes before anything else runs. IP allowlisting is defence in depth and never a substitute.

Why vnp_TxnRef must be unique per attempt, not per order

VNPay treats the reference as the identity of the transaction. Reusing it after a failed attempt — the natural thing to do, since it is the same order — is rejected as a duplicate, so the customer who fails once can never pay. Checkpoint

An order is 150,000 VND. What goes in vnp_Amount?

15000000. VNPay defines the field as amount × 100 for every currency, including VND which has no minor unit. Do the multiplication in the VNPay adapter only, and divide before comparing when the callback arrives.

Your integration works locally and fails every transaction in production. What would you check first?

The timezone of vnp_CreateDate. It must be GMT+7, so a UTC server sends a timestamp seven hours in the past and every request is rejected as expired — while a developer machine set to Vietnam time works perfectly. A customer’s payment fails and they try again with the same vnp_TxnRef. What happens?

VNPay rejects it as a duplicate transaction reference, so that customer can never complete the order. The reference identifies an attempt, not an order — use <order>-<attempt> and map it back on the callback.

Which response code must not be treated as a plain failure?

07 — suspected fraud, funds already deducted, under review. Treating it as failed and inviting the customer to pay again produces a double charge. 24 also deserves specific handling: it means the customer cancelled, which is not an error and should return them to the cart.

Which is the argument for an adapter per gateway

Checkpoint

What is the most dangerous difference between the two gateways?

The amount convention. VNPay wants the amount × 100; PayOS wants plain dong. A shared amount helper across both charges a hundred times too much or too little, and neither produces an error — the transaction simply succeeds for the wrong sum. Keep the conversion inside each adapter.

Which part of a PayOS webhook does the signature cover?

The data object only, not the envelope. Verifying over the whole body fails, and reading code from the outer level trusts an unsigned field. Reconstruct the signing string from body.data, verify, then read the outcome from body.data.code — the inner one.

How does PayOS signing differ from VNPay’s beyond the hash algorithm?

No url-encoding — values are signed raw, so spaces stay spaces where VNPay uses +. Nulls become empty strings rather than being dropped, nested values are JSON-stringified, and the sorted pairs are joined plainly. Same shape, different bytes at every step.

Why store PayOS’s reference field?

It is the bank’s own transfer reference and appears on the merchant statement, which makes it the join key for reconciliation. An integration that discards it has thrown away the only field that ties its records to the bank’s.

Chapter Six · Part IV

Idempotency and Retries

Every message in a payment system can arrive twice, and every request you send can succeed while the response is lost. Those two facts make idempotency not a refinement but the property the whole design rests on — because the alternative to handling duplicates is charging somebody twice.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Say why duplicates are guaranteed rather than unlikely
  • Make a callback handler safe to run repeatedly
  • Enforce idempotency where it cannot be bypassed
  • Send idempotent requests to a gateway
  • Retry without making things worse

6.1 Duplicates are normal traffic

CauseProduces
The gateway retries an unacknowledged callbackThe same event, again
Your acknowledgement was lostThe same event, again
A deploy interrupted processingA partially applied event, then a retry
The customer pressed back and paid againTwo genuine payments
Your own retry after a timeoutA second request for one intent
Two workers took the same jobConcurrent processing of one event

Table 6.1 Only the fourth row is a second payment; the rest are one payment described more than once. The handler has to tell them apart, and the field that does it is the gateway’s own transaction reference — the same reference means the same money, however many times you are told about it.

6.2 Where to enforce it

Why SELECT-then-INSERT is not idempotency

Because there is a gap between the two statements, and under concurrency both callers see “not present” and both insert. It works in testing, where callbacks arrive one at a time, and fails under exactly the conditions that produce duplicates in the first place — a retry storm, a redeploy, two workers on one queue. Let the constraint decide; it is the only participant that sees both attempts.

6.3 Idempotent requests, not just handlers

OperationNaturally idempotent?If not
Reading a payment statusYes—
Creating a payment linkNoDerive the reference from the order
Capturing an authorisationNoIdempotency key
RefundingNoIdempotency key, always
CancellingUsuallyCheck state first
Applying a callbackNo§6.2

Table 6.2 Refunds are the row to be careful with, because the failure is expensive in the direction nobody notices: a duplicate refund sends the customer money twice and nothing in your system complains. A duplicate charge generates a support ticket within a day; a duplicate refund is found during reconciliation, if at all.

And when in doubt, ask before you retry

Every gateway offers a query API — VNPay’s querydr, PayOS’s payment-link lookup. When a request times out and you do not know whether it landed, query the transaction rather than repeating it. Reading is always safe; writing again may not be. This is also the mechanism Chapter 8 uses to find payments whose callback never arrived at all.

6.4 Retrying without making it worse

RuleBecause
Exponential backoff with jitterSynchronised retries are a second outage

Table 6.3 The last two rows matter more here than in ordinary systems. In most services a permanently failed job is an inconvenience; in payments it is a customer who paid and received nothing, or a refund that was never sent. The dead-letter queue is where those land, and if nobody is alerted on it, the failure is indistinguishable from success until the customer complains.

The one case where you must not retry automatically

A request that may have partially succeeded and has no idempotency key. Retrying might duplicate it; not retrying might lose it. The correct action is neither: record it for manual review, query the gateway for the true state, and reconcile. This situation is entirely avoidable, which is the argument for putting an idempotency key on every mutating call from the beginning rather than adding them after the first incident.

Checkpoint

Why are duplicate callbacks guaranteed rather than unlikely?

Because acknowledgement is unreliable: the gateway retries anything it did not hear back about, and your response can be lost after the work was done. Add deploys interrupting processing and two workers taking one job, and duplicates are ordinary traffic. Only a customer genuinely paying twice is a second payment; the rest are one payment described repeatedly.

Why is a unique constraint better than checking whether the event exists?

Because SELECT-then-INSERT has a gap: under concurrency both callers see nothing and both insert. The constraint is evaluated by the database across all processes, so exactly one insert succeeds — and it keeps working when someone adds another code path later.

Why must an idempotency key be derived rather than randomly generated?

Because a retry must present the same key as the attempt it is retrying. A fresh UUID per call makes every retry a distinct request, which is exactly what the key was supposed to prevent. Derive it from identifiers you already have, such as the order and attempt ids.

A refund request times out. Do you retry it?

Only if it carried an idempotency key. Otherwise query the gateway for the transaction’s true state — reading is always safe — and reconcile. A duplicate refund sends money twice and produces no complaint from anybody, so it is found late or never.

Chapter Seven · Part IV

Refunds and Cancellations

Sending money back is the operation with the least test coverage and the most ways to go wrong, because every rehearsal costs real money. It is also the one where a bug is invisible: a duplicate charge produces a complaint within a day, and a duplicate refund produces silence.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Distinguish a void, a refund and a chargeback
  • Refund exactly once, under retries
  • Handle partial refunds and the arithmetic they need
  • Model refund state so it can be reconciled
  • Decide what to do when a refund fails

7.1 Three different operations

Void / cancelRefundChargeback
WhenBefore settlementAfter settlementCustomer disputes
Initiated byYouYouThe customer’s bank
Customer seesThe hold disappearsMoney returns in daysMoney returns
FeeUsually noneOften the original fee is keptA penalty fee
Reversible—NoYou can contest it

Table 7.1 The last row is the practical shape of this chapter for Vietnamese gateways: bank-transfer and QR payments settle immediately, so there is generally no authorisation to void — the only instrument is a refund. That removes a category of bug and means every correction costs a real transfer and, usually, the fee on the original.

And a refund is not the inverse of a payment

It is a second transaction, with its own reference, its own fee treatment, its own settlement delay, and its own ways to fail. Modelling it as “set status back to unpaid” loses all of that, and produces an order table that cannot answer how much money actually moved or when. A refund is a row, not a state change.

7.2 Refunding exactly once

The window between the call and the update

If the process dies after the gateway succeeds and before the row is updated, you have a pending refund that actually completed. That is why the idempotency key exists and why Chapter 8 exists: a job that finds refunds stuck in pending and asks the gateway what happened is the only thing that can resolve it correctly. Retrying blindly risks a second refund; assuming failure loses one.

7.3 Partial refunds, and the arithmetic

QuestionDecide once
Refunding one line of fiveRefund the line total, or a share of the order total?

Table 7.2 Row five is worth planning around: many gateways keep the original transaction fee when you refund, so a fully refunded order leaves the merchant out of pocket by the fee. That is a business decision to make deliberately — some shops absorb it, some make it a restocking charge — and either way the ledger in Chapter 8 has to record it, because the money genuinely did not come back.

Refund in minor units, never in percentages

“Refund 33%” of 150,000 across three lines produces three roundings that need not sum to the total refunded, and the discrepancy shows up as an order that can never be fully refunded — a few dong short forever. Compute each refund as an integer number of dong, sum them, and make the last one absorb the remainder so the total is exact.

Table 7.3 The last row is why this chapter is careful. There is no mechanism to reverse a refund — the money has been sent, and recovering it depends entirely on the customer’s goodwill. It is the one operation in the whole system with no undo, which is the argument for the idempotency key, the over-refund check, and a second pair of eyes on anything large.

And keep refunds out of the automatic path where you can

Automatic refunds are convenient and they are also an automated way to send money to strangers. A rule with a bug — a retry loop, a mis-parsed webhook, an inverted condition — refunds continuously until somebody notices the bank balance. If refunds must be automatic, cap them: per transaction, per hour, and in total per day, with the cap enforced in code rather than in a policy document, and an alert when it is approached.

Checkpoint

Why is a refund not simply reversing the order status?

Because it is a second transaction with its own reference, fee treatment, settlement delay and failure modes. Modelling it as a state change loses the record of how much money actually moved and when, which is exactly what reconciliation needs. A refund is a row.

Which check is most often missing from a partial-refund implementation?

Summing all prior non-failed refunds before allowing another. Three partial refunds of 60,000 against a 150,000 order are each individually valid and collectively 30,000 too much. Check the running total, not the most recent refund.

A refund request times out. Retry?

No — query the gateway for its state. A duplicate refund sends the customer money twice and generates no complaint, so it is found late or never. If the call carried a derived idempotency key a retry is safe; without one, query and reconcile.

Why cap automatic refunds in code?

Because an automatic refund path is an automated way to send money to strangers, and a bug in it — an inverted condition, a retry loop, a mis-parsed webhook — keeps sending until a human notices the bank balance. There is no mechanism to reverse a refund, so the cap must be enforced where it cannot be bypassed.

Chapter Eight · Part V

Reconciliation and Ledgers

Reconciliation is the process of discovering that your records and the gateway’s disagree, which they will. Webhooks are lost, refunds hang, fees appear that nobody recorded, and a payment settles under a reference your database has never seen. A system without reconciliation does not have fewer discrepancies — it has the same number, undetected.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Record money as a ledger rather than as a status
  • Prove your books are internally consistent
  • Compare your records against the gateway
  • Classify and resolve the differences you find
  • Find the payments whose callback never arrived

8.1 A status field is not an accounting record

order.status = "paid" cannot answerA ledger can

Table 8.1 A status field records that something happened; a ledger records what moved, from where, to where. The last row is the difference that matters — in double-entry bookkeeping every event is written as two or more entries that sum to zero, so a discrepancy is not merely visible, it makes the books refuse to balance.

8.2 Comparing against the gateway

It is a customer who paid, whose webhook was lost during a deploy or exhausted its retries, and who is waiting for something you do not know you owe them. Nothing in your own system can detect it — by definition, your system has no record — so the only way to find it is to ask the gateway what it thinks happened.

And the last row is why naive comparisons cry wolf

Payments made near midnight settle the following day, refunds settle on their own schedule, and gateways report in their own timezone. A reconciliation that compares by your created-at timestamp will report dozens of false discrepancies daily and be switched off within a week. Compare on the gateway’s settlement date, allow a window on either side, and match on the reference rather than on time.

A ledger entry with neither is indistinguishable from a bug.

The measure of whether reconciliation is working

Not zero discrepancies — you will always have some, because timing and fees guarantee it. The measure is that every discrepancy is explained and closed within a day, and that the count is not trending upward. A steady handful is a healthy process; a growing backlog means something changed and nobody noticed, and the backlog is where a lost customer payment will be hiding.

Checkpoint

Why is order.status insufficient as a financial record?

Because it records that something happened, not what moved. It cannot say how much was received, how much the gateway kept in fees, what has been paid out, or what is still owed — and it cannot be checked for internal consistency. A double-entry ledger sums to zero, so a discrepancy makes the books refuse to balance rather than hiding in a column.

Which reconciliation discrepancy is most urgent, and why can only the gateway reveal it?

A payment the gateway has that you do not. It is a customer who paid and is waiting for something you have no record of — so nothing in your own system can detect it, by definition. Only comparing against the gateway’s report finds it.

Why must the sweeper query the gateway rather than expire stale orders?

Because a pending order is not evidence of failure — it may be a successful payment whose webhook was lost. Ask, and apply the answer through the same idempotent path a webhook would use, so a late callback cannot double-apply it. Expiring without asking silently discards customers’ money.

What is the right target for a reconciliation process?

Not zero discrepancies — timing and fees guarantee some. The target is that every discrepancy is explained and closed within a day and the count is not trending upward. A growing backlog is where a lost customer payment will be hiding, and a silently failed reconciliation job is worse than none because everyone believes it is running.

The security of a payment integration is mostly a question of what you decline to hold. Card data you never receive cannot leak, an amount you never trust cannot be altered, and a secret that only exists in one place cannot be found in a repository. This chapter is about arranging things so the dangerous options are not available.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to
  • Keep card data out of your systems, and know what that buys
  • Protect the secrets the whole scheme depends on
  • Close the tampering routes an attacker will try
  • Apply sensible controls without pretending to be a fraud team

What you may keep

The last four digits, the brand, the expiry month and year, and the gateway’s token for the card. Between them they cover the receipt, the “pay with the card ending 1111” screen, and future charges — all without you ever holding a number that could be used elsewhere. If a feature seems to require the full PAN, it almost always requires the gateway’s token instead.

That single string is the difference between a payment system and a free shop.

And keep the sandbox and production secrets genuinely apart

Chapter 4 made this a configuration point; it is also a security one. A sandbox secret that reaches production causes signature failures, which is merely embarrassing. A production secret in a developer’s .env, in a shared document, or in a CI log is a real exposure — and it is the most common way these keys actually leak, far ahead of anything an attacker does.

The last row is not hypothetical

It is the same class of bug as the fourth, arriving from the other direction: the customer pays a smaller amount than the order, the gateway reports a perfectly valid successful payment for that smaller amount, and a handler that checks only vnp_ResponseCode == "00" marks the order paid. Bank-transfer flows, which dominate in Vietnam, carry much less fraud risk of this kind to begin with.

Do not build a fraud engine you cannot staff

Every rule produces false positives, and a false positive is a real customer who cannot buy. A blocking rule with no queue behind it and nobody to review it is a rule that silently loses sales, and nobody will attribute the missing revenue to it. Start by logging what a rule would have blocked, look at those cases for a fortnight, and only then decide whether the rule should act — and who is going to look at what it catches.

The one habit that covers most of this chapter

Never trust a number that arrived from outside. Not the amount in a form, not the total in a callback, not the price in a URL, not the currency in a webhook. Compare each against something you computed and stored, and reject on any mismatch. Signatures establish that a message was not altered in transit; they say nothing about whether it is about the order you think, for the amount you charged. That comparison is one line of code and it closes the majority of the ways a payment integration is actually attacked.

Checkpoint

What decides how much PCI compliance work you face?

Whether cardholder data touches your systems. A redirect flow — which is how VNPay and PayOS work — means it never does, keeping you in the smallest scope. Handling card data on your own servers moves you to the largest, which is months of work plus an audit rather than days.

Your gateway hash secret is committed to a repository. What must you do?

Rotate it at the gateway. Removing the commit does not help — it exists in every clone, fork and CI cache that fetched it. Until it is rotated, anyone holding it can post a signed callback claiming any order is paid, because that secret is the entire authentication on your IPN endpoint.

A signature verifies. Can you mark the order paid?

Not yet. The signature proves the message was not altered in transit; it does not prove the message concerns your order, for the amount you charged. Compare the callback’s amount and order reference against your own stored record, and check you have not already applied it.

Why log what a fraud rule would have blocked before enabling it?

Because every rule produces false positives, and a false positive is a real customer who cannot buy — a loss nobody attributes to the rule. Watching what it would have caught for a couple of weeks tells you the true positive rate and, just as importantly, whether anyone is available to review what it catches.
